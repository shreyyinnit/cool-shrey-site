Why Every Brand Sounds the Same

The homogenization of corporate voice and what it means for authentic communication.

Apr 26, 2026The Motion DeskMotion Desk
Explore The Gazette

There is a tone you will recognize immediately if you have spent any time on the internet in the last decade. It is warm but not intimate. Casual but not careless. Self-aware but not self-deprecating to the point of undermining confidence. It uses sentence fragments for emphasis. It asks rhetorical questions and then answers them. It is relentlessly, exhaustingly positive — about its product, about its customers, about the act of existing as a brand in what it will almost certainly describe, at some point, as this space.

This tone has no single author. It emerged, like most cultural phenomena, from a convergence of incentives, anxieties, and imitations that compounded over time until what had once been a choice became an assumption. It is the voice of the startup, the DTC brand, the challenger category disruptor, the purpose-driven organization on a mission to change the way you think about oat milk, mattresses, luggage, eyeglasses, or razors. It is, increasingly, the voice of legacy corporations attempting to seem less like legacy corporations. It is everywhere, and it is — if you listen carefully — saying almost nothing at all.

This is a story about how that happened, why it persists, and what the homogenization of corporate voice costs us — not just aesthetically, but in the more fundamental currency of trust.

I. The Sound of Safety

To understand why brands converge on a single voice, you have to understand what brand voice actually is and why it terrifies the people responsible for it.

A brand's voice is, in theory, the expression of a coherent identity — the set of values, personality traits, and aesthetic commitments that distinguish one company from another. In practice, it is often the output of a committee process designed primarily to avoid saying anything that could be criticized. The language of brand guidelines — terms like "approachable," "authentic," "human," "conversational" — reads less like description and more like aspiration, a list of things a brand wishes it were allowed to be if only the legal team, the compliance department, and the senior vice president of something would let it.

The result is voice by subtraction. You remove anything too specific (it might alienate someone), anything too opinionated (it might offend someone), anything too strange (it might confuse someone), and anything too formal (it might seem out of touch). What remains, after all that removal, is a kind of residue — not quite personality, not quite voice, but the shape left behind when personality has been carefully excised.

This residue has a sound. It is the sound of brands on Twitter telling you they "heard you" after a service outage. It is the sound of airlines assuring you they are "committed to doing better." It is the sound of a snack company tweeting "we see you, we love you, take a break" during a news event that has nothing to do with snacks. It is the sound of every company that ever called its customers a "community," its product a "solution," and its founding story a "journey."

The vocabulary of this voice is finite and well-documented. Authentic. Transparent. Values-driven. Customer-obsessed. Human-centric. Passionate. Purpose-led. Storytelling. These words appear in so many brand documents that they have become, through sheer repetition, semantically empty — signifiers that no longer signify because they have been stretched past the point where they can hold meaning. When every brand is authentic, the word authentic describes nothing.

II. The Millennial Pivot and Its Afterlife

The homogenization of brand voice has a fairly traceable origin point, or at least a significant inflection: the rise of direct-to-consumer brands in the early 2010s and the particular aesthetic sensibility they brought with them.

Companies like Warby Parker, Everlane, Casper, and Glossier were doing something genuinely new in the context of consumer brands: they were selling directly through the internet, which meant they had to build relationships with customers entirely through communication. There was no store experience, no salesperson, no physical object you could pick up and examine. There was only the website, the email, the Instagram post. Voice became the product, in a way it had never quite been before.

These brands were also selling to a particular demographic — younger consumers who had grown up online, who were skeptical of advertising in its traditional forms, and who had developed fairly sophisticated radar for what they perceived as inauthenticity. The solution the DTC category landed on was a voice that performed the absence of corporate calculation: conversational, slightly irreverent, apparently candid about the brand's own limitations and the absurdity of its position as a company trying to sell you something. The irony was built in. We know you know this is marketing. We're doing it anyway, but we're doing it with a wink.

It worked. For a while, it worked remarkably well. Glossier built a beauty company that felt, to a significant portion of its audience, less like a brand than a friend. Everlane's radical transparency — publishing the cost breakdown of each garment — felt like a genuine rupture with the opacity of the fashion industry. The voice was inseparable from the value proposition. The tone was the trust.

But then something predictable happened: every other brand noticed. The aesthetic diffused. The wink became a convention. By the middle of the decade, legacy brands were retooling their communications to sound like startups, startups were trying to out-casual each other, and the entire mode had arrived at the fate of all successful aesthetic innovations: it became a style sheet you could download rather than an expression of something real.

The voice that had signified authenticity became, through its ubiquity, the most reliable signal of inauthenticity. The wink lost its meaning when everyone was winking.

III. The Algorithm Shaped the Message

The homogenization of brand voice was accelerated by something most brand managers would rather not discuss too openly: the platforms shaped the message.

When Facebook's algorithm began rewarding content that generated emotional engagement, brands learned to write emotionally. When LinkedIn's algorithm began surfacing posts that told personal stories — the "I failed and here's what I learned" genre, the "I was in an elevator with a stranger and they changed my life" genre — brands and brand-adjacent executives began writing in that register. When Twitter rewarded wit and brevity and the appearance of spontaneity, brands hired social media managers tasked with performing spontaneity at scale. When TikTok rewarded relatability and self-deprecation and a particular kind of chaotic energy, brands attempted to perform those qualities too, with results that ranged from occasionally charming to deeply uncomfortable.

The platforms were not asking brands to sound the same. They were asking brands to optimize for engagement, and the emergent result of every brand optimizing for engagement on the same platforms was convergence. The medium was shaping the message in exactly the way Marshall McLuhan would have predicted, except McLuhan could not have predicted that the medium would be a set of engagement-maximizing algorithms with no aesthetic preferences of their own.

What the algorithm rewards is not voice — it has no ability to evaluate voice — but performance. Specifically, the performance of certain emotional states: warmth, enthusiasm, relatability, vulnerability, conviction. Brands learned to perform these states because performing them produced engagement, and engagement produced reach, and reach produced sales or at least the metrics that justified the social media budget. The performance became the voice, and the voice became indistinguishable from every other performance of the same emotional states on the same platforms.

There is a term in linguistics — register — that describes the variety of language appropriate to a particular social situation. We all shift register constantly: the way you speak to your grandmother is different from the way you speak to your employer, which is different from the way you speak to your closest friend. What the social media era did to brand communication was collapse register into a single mode: the mode of the friend, always and everywhere, regardless of context or appropriateness. The brand that used to send you formal letters now texts you like a peer. The airline that used to issue press releases now slides into your mentions. The register is the same whether it is delivering good news or apologizing for losing your luggage.

This has the effect, over time, of making the friendliness feel threatening. Familiarity without relationship is a kind of aggression — a demand for intimacy that hasn't been earned.

IV. Purpose, and the Collapse of Purpose

The 2010s also brought, alongside the DTC aesthetic revolution, the rise of brand purpose — the idea that companies needed to stand for something beyond their products if they wanted to connect with consumers who had grown suspicious of pure commercial motivation.

In its most serious form, this was a legitimate reconsideration of corporate responsibility. A company that genuinely operates with a coherent ethical framework, that makes decisions consistent with stated values even when it costs money, is doing something real. Patagonia's commitment to environmental advocacy — expressed through its product, its supply chain, its political spending, and its eventual decision to restructure its ownership specifically to protect the company's mission — is a case study in what purpose actually looks like when it informs behavior rather than just communication.

But purpose as a communication strategy, disconnected from the behavior it is supposed to describe, is something different entirely. It is the company that announces its commitment to sustainability while operating a supply chain that produces enormous environmental harm. It is the brand that declares its support for social justice in a tweet while its labor practices remain unchanged. It is the purpose statement as aesthetic object — the values page on the website, the mission paragraph in the annual report, the Instagram post with a black square or a rainbow flag or a mental health awareness ribbon, depending on the calendar.

When purpose is decorative rather than operational, it reads as what it is: a bid for trust that hasn't been earned through the conduct that trust requires. And audiences — particularly younger audiences who grew up with enough media literacy to recognize the gap between statement and behavior — tend to clock this fairly quickly. The backlash to purpose-washing (a term that barely needed to be invented, given how predictably the pattern recurs) has been loud enough that some brands have retreated from explicit purpose messaging. But the voice remains. The warmth, the values-adjacency, the performance of care — these persist even as the explicit purpose claims become more hedged.

What gets lost in all of this is the possibility of corporate communication that is simply honest about what it is. A company that makes a good product and wants you to buy it, and says so directly, has a kind of integrity that the elaborate performance of purpose and community and mission tends to undermine. Directness is its own form of respect. The implication that a customer needs to be told that a brand cares, rather than simply shown a product worth buying at a price that is fair, is a kind of condescension dressed up as warmth.

V. The Copywriter and the Content Machine

Behind all of this is a labor story that rarely gets told when people discuss brand voice.

Corporate communication is produced by people — writers, strategists, social media managers, content producers — who are themselves operating within institutional constraints that shape what they are able to say. The copywriter who would prefer to write something strange and specific and genuinely funny is working within a brief that specifies approved vocabulary, tone guidelines, legal review requirements, and the preferences of multiple stakeholders who have veto power over the final product. The social media manager who wants to respond to a customer complaint with something honest and direct is navigating a policy document that has been written to protect the company from liability, not to enable authentic communication.

The people who produce brand voice are often quite good at writing. The institutional processes that mediate between their ability and the final output are frequently terrible at producing writing that sounds like a person wrote it. The voice that emerges from the process — warm, careful, slightly anonymous — is the voice of the process itself.

This is not a new observation. Don DeLillo noticed something like it in White Noise, where the corporate-speak that surrounds his characters functions as a kind of white noise, semantically dense and informationally empty. What is new is the scale. The content machine that modern brands operate — the daily Instagram posts, the email newsletters, the LinkedIn articles, the Twitter threads, the TikTok videos, the podcast episodes, the blog posts that serve SEO functions while maintaining the fiction of human interest — requires volume that individual human writers cannot sustain. The result is content produced to fill a schedule rather than to say something, voice maintained as consistency rather than as expression.

Generative AI has, in the last few years, made this dynamic both more efficient and more visible. The same homogenized voice that emerged from human institutional processes can now be produced at scale without human labor — which is convenient for the brands producing it, but which raises the obvious question of what the voice was for in the first place. If a brand's communication can be fully generated by a language model trained on the corpus of all previous brand communication, then the voice was never an expression of identity. It was always just a style — reproducible, transferable, fundamentally empty.

VI. What Differentiation Actually Requires

There are brands that sound like themselves. They are not common, and what distinguishes them is not a cleverer brief or a better agency. It is something more fundamental: a willingness to be specific.

Specificity is the enemy of the homogenized brand voice because specificity requires commitment. To say something specific is to say something that is true for you and potentially not true for someone else — which means potentially alienating someone, which is what institutional communication processes are designed to prevent. The warmth and the relatability and the purpose language are all forms of maximum inclusivity, attempts to speak to everyone that end up speaking to no one in particular.

The brands that manage to sound distinct tend to have one or more of the following: a founder who writes and whose personality is specific enough to resist homogenization; a product category where the conventions are absurd enough to invite parody; an audience small and particular enough that speaking to everyone is not a viable strategy; or institutional leadership with enough confidence in the brand's identity to resist the pull toward safety.

Innocent Drinks, the British smoothie company, built a voice so distinct — warm, genuinely funny, slightly eccentric, willing to be boring about things that were actually boring — that it became a case study in marketing education. It helped that the founders wrote early communications themselves, and that the category (juice) was absurd enough that self-awareness was the only dignified response. Cards Against Humanity has built a brand almost entirely on the refusal to perform warmth — the corporate communications are as misanthropic as the product, and the consistency is itself the brand identity.

These are exceptions rather than rules, and they resist systemization. You cannot produce a genuine voice by reverse-engineering what makes another voice genuine. The process is the problem: the moment you design for authenticity, you have departed from it.

VII. The Cost of Sameness

The homogenization of corporate voice is easy to dismiss as a minor aesthetic irritation — the background noise of commercial culture, easily ignored. But it has costs that extend beyond aesthetics.

Trust is built through specific, accurate, consistent communication over time. When brand voice is generic, the signals it sends are generic — and generic signals are indistinguishable from signals that have been engineered to produce a particular response regardless of whether that response is warranted. A brand that says "we care about you" in the same warm, practiced tone whether it is announcing a new product or responding to a data breach has trained its audience to distrust the warmth. The tone becomes the tell.

The broader cost is to the public language of commercial life. We spend enormous amounts of time in environments produced by corporate communication — on apps, in stores, through advertising, on packaging. The voice of those environments shapes, to a degree we rarely reckon with, the vocabulary and register available to us for ordinary communication. When that voice is systematically emptied of meaning — when authentic means nothing, when community means nothing, when values means nothing — the words become unavailable for uses where they might mean something.

Language is not a fixed resource, but it is not inexhaustible either. Words that have been used too many times to mean too many things without consequence stop doing the work they were built for. The homogenization of brand voice is, at its outermost edge, a small contribution to a larger poverty — the poverty of a language in which the words for sincerity have been so thoroughly colonized by performance that sincerity has no reliable way to announce itself.

This is the real cost of every brand sounding the same. Not that the marketing is boring — it is, but that is survivable. The cost is that it becomes harder to hear when someone actually means it.
